The Generalization Cliff

When a general-purpose embedding model tops a public leaderboard, that tells you very little about how it will do on your data. The gap between "good on MTEB" and "good on your queries" is not a gentle slope, it is a cliff. Let's explore how far models fall (BRIGHT), why it happens (benchmark contamination), and what fixes it (domain fine-tuning), and why that fix has its own cliff.

The collapse in one number

BRIGHT shows it most clearly. A model that scores 59.0 nDCG@10 on MTEB scores 18.3 on BRIGHT. That is a collapse, not a degradation. BRIGHT's queries come from StackExchange, LeetCode, and math competitions, and the relevant document often doesn't look related at all. It might prove the theorem the answer depends on, or solve a different problem with the same algorithm. You only see the connection once you've worked out the answer. Dense embedding models, whatever else they do well, don't reason about relevance in several steps. Semantic similarity is all a Bi-Encoder can express, and once the link between query and document stops being lexical or topical, similarity is a poor stand-in for "this document helps answer this question."

Ordinary queries hit it too

BRIGHT is the extreme case. The same drop appears on everyday in-domain retrieval where no reasoning is needed. MTEB and BEIR measure general-domain performance and are heavily optimized against, so a model's leaderboard position reflects how well it fits the benchmark about as much as how good it is (more on this in my notes on model selection and fine-tuning evaluation). The board leader routinely loses to a mid-ranked model on in-domain data. A model can be excellent at general text similarity and mediocre on your catalog or your legal corpus at the same time; those are different skills, and public benchmarks don't separate them.

Why: contamination, and an incentive that moves

RTEB names the mechanism: public benchmarks reward memorization. When the corpus, queries, and labels are all public, they leak into training data, and a model that has effectively seen the test posts a score that falls apart on new data. RTEB splits its datasets into an open half and a private half held by the maintainers, which makes the gap measurable. If a model scores well on the open datasets and drops sharply on the private ones, it has been trained on the test.

RTEB's governance history is the more interesting part. In 2026 the private column was temporarily removed after concerns that vendors with access to the private data had a structural advantage. Keeping a test set secret moves the incentive problem somewhere else. Someone has to hold the secret, and if that party also competes, the benchmark trades contamination for a conflict of interest.

BEIR made an earlier version of this critique by training models on one corpus (usually MS MARCO) and evaluating them zero-shot on eighteen unrelated ones. Its most durable finding, that BM25 is a stubbornly strong zero-shot baseline, fits the same picture: a method with no learned generalization to fail has nothing to fall off.

Fine-tuning closes most of the gap

The direct response is domain fine-tuning, and two worked examples show how much it recovers.

Shaw Talebi's tutorial uses a medical Q&A task. Asked "What causes hypertension?", a general all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model retrieves articles about blood pressure in general, which are similar in everyday English but not medically relevant, and misses documents that answer the question in specialized medical terminology. Fine-tuning on domain triplets with hard negatives closes much of that gap. The reported NDCG@10 gains are 12 to 18%, with diminishing returns past roughly 10,000 to 20,000 examples.

Thierry Damiba's SPLADE series gives numbers for sparse retrieval. Off-the-shelf SPLADE, trained on MS MARCO web search, beats BM25 on an e-commerce catalog by only 7.2% nDCG@10. Fine-tuned on catalog data, the same architecture reaches +27.5% over BM25, nearly four times the improvement, from domain adaptation alone and with no change to the architecture.

Fine-tuning has a cliff of its own

What fine-tuning produces is a domain model, and Damiba's series is explicit about the cost. Tested on other catalogs, the SPLADE model fine-tuned on ESCI loses to the off-the-shelf baseline on Home Depot data (0.384 vs 0.391 nDCG@10), so training on one retailer's catalog doesn't automatically carry over to a neighboring one. On general web queries (MS MARCO) the fine-tuned model falls well below BM25. That is catastrophic forgetting: Amazon-specific vocabulary overwrote general language ability. Blending several catalogs in multi-domain training gives up some peak in-domain performance in exchange for consistency across them, so it picks a different point on the tradeoff instead of escaping it. The generalization cliff does not disappear when you fine-tune; it just moves, from "general model, your domain" to "your domain, someone else's domain."

What to do about it

  • Use MTEB and BEIR to build a shortlist, and make the final pick on your own relevance judgments. I collect the options in Retrieval Benchmarks and Leaderboards.
  • Where a leaderboard reports both open and private scores, treat a drop between them as diagnostic. It is stronger evidence of overfitting than absolute position.
  • If you have domain data, fine-tune. The recovery is large, and it comes from training on your own data, not from a new architecture. Hard negatives add to it: in Damiba's series, mining them was worth another 5–10% on top of basic fine-tuning.
  • Expect a fine-tuned model to get worse outside its domain, and choose single-domain or multi-domain training depending on whether you serve one catalog or several.
