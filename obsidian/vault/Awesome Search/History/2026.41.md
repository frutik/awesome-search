## 2026-10-11 — Cheap signals for e-commerce ranking (1 new, 0 updated)

Many of the fixes that last in [[E-commerce Search|e-commerce search]] are built from data the shop already has: conversion logs, click positions, stock counts and session rephrasings. [[Cheap Signals, Shipped]] is [[Andrew Kornilov]]'s guide to Marcos Rebelo's seven-post series making that case. The series covers query-to-category priors that filter retrieval and guard vector search ([[Query Classification]]), a click score corrected for [[Position Bias|position bias]], a stock [[Results Boosting|boost]] measured in days of stock, sold-out items that fade instead of vanishing, [[Synonyms|synonyms]] mined from session reformulations, and a weekly review of the queries losing the most clicks. The original posts are member-only on Medium, so only the ideas are summarised here.

## 2026-10-11 — Voyage rerank-3 and the long-document push (4 new, 10 updated)

Reranker traffic is changing: coding agents now send many of the queries, and the candidate documents are getting longer. [[Voyage AI]]'s [[Voyage rerank-3]] and rerank-3-lite target both. They keep rerank-2.5's price, 32K context and instruction-following, and keep its score distributions, so tuned thresholds survive the upgrade ([[Calibrated Relevance Probability]]). On Voyage's own 95-dataset suite, rerank-3 leads [[Cohere]] Rerank v4.0 Pro by 2.72% NDCG@10, and by 13.84% on [[LongEmbed]] long documents. A partial, untuned comparison has it ahead of [[Jev]] in code, law and finance. Every figure comes from the vendor's launch post.

- [[Hev meets Jev]], [[Hev]], [[Reception of Jev]] and [[Voyage AI]] called Voyage rerank-3 the best reranker in Hev's comparison → the published post's leader is Mixedbread's mxbai-rerank-v3.1-listwise at 0.511; untuned Jev is 0.003 behind rerank-3 and 0.010 behind the leader.

## 2026-10-11 — Calibrating an AI judge before trusting it (2 new, 8 updated)

How good an AI relevance judge is depends on the model, the prompt, the collection and the reference it is compared with, all together. [[Judge Calibration]] covers measuring a judge against a reference that has already rated a frozen random sample of pairs. Agreement is reported both per pair, with Krippendorff's α, and per ranking, with [[Kendall Rank Correlation|Kendall's τ]] over per-query [[NDCG|nDCG@10]], because the two [[Levels of Judge Agreement|levels]] decouple. Prompt variants are compared on the same pairs. With escalating judges, calibration runs as a chain from people to the expensive judge to the cheap one ([[Staged Judging]]). From [[Andrew Kornilov]]'s essay on a [[Quepid]] calibration tab that was still a proposal when the essay was written.

## 2026-10-11 — Jev beyond the single relevance question (7 new, 16 updated)

Search work with [[Jev]] has moved past asking one "is this relevant?" question. [[Elastic]] asks for [[ESCI Label Scheme|ESCI]]-relationship probabilities plus constraint signals and lets a short policy score them. On 250 [[Amazon ESCI Dataset|ESCI]] queries, where random order already scores 0.86, nDCG@10 rose from 0.9351 to 0.9565. [[Doug Turnbull]] has an LLM write a yes/no rubric for each query and sums the answers ([[Bag of Decisions]]). [[Qdrant]] applies the same model to [[MMR]] diversity, a tiered category filter/boost, and topic-boundary [[Text Chunking|chunking]] evaluated on [[QASPER]]. Qdrant also shows *apple* filtered to food at 0.91 confidence. Each is a single experiment run by its authors on their own benchmarks.

## 2026-10-05 — Semantic and outcome-based relevance compared (1 new, 6 updated)

Whether a result matches the query and whether people buy it are different questions, and the answers can move apart: at [[Etsy]], engagement sometimes falls as semantic relevance rises. [[Outcome-Based vs Semantic Relevance]] sets the two side by side. It covers where each label comes from (judges and [[ESCI Label Scheme|ESCI]] grades against clicks and conversions), why they diverge (popularity, [[Position Bias]], items nobody saw), and four ways systems combine them. Semantic match can gate candidates that commerce signals then order, as at [[Criteo]]. [[Learning to Rank]] can blend text-match features under behavioural labels, where the choice of label decides which relevance wins. Clicks can train a semantic model, and behaviour can decide which pairs get judged at all.

## 2026-10-05 — Outcome-based relevance and Criteo's CLEPR (6 new, 14 updated)

A product can match a query on brand, category and attributes and still not be what shoppers buy. [[Outcome-Based Relevance]] covers relevance judged by clicks and purchases, set against [[Semantic Relevance]], and [[CLEPR]] covers [[Criteo]]'s two-tower query–product model, trained on clicks rebuilt from session logs. CLEPR gates sponsored-product ads on semantic match. In Criteo's offline test over 400 retrieved candidates, re-ranking by recent sales share raised the purchased product's normalized rank score about 2.5×. From posts by [[Maxime Vono]] and [[Paul Coursaux]]; both are member-only, and every figure is Criteo's own.
