## 2026-11-14 — llm-search-eval, and judge calibration in Quepid (1 new, 2 updated)

Gold labels run out when a new retriever surfaces products nobody judged, and a small metric gain over a few dozen queries can be chance. [[llm-search-eval]], an MIT-licensed Python tool first released in October 2026, fills unlabeled pairs with [[LLM as Judge|LLM judges]] while keeping gold labels authoritative, measures agreement within judge panels and against gold, and attaches a paired randomization test and bootstrap intervals to every system comparison, with a CI gate on top. [[Offline Search Evaluation Tools]] now covers it alongside [[Quepid]]'s proposed Judge Calibration tab, which reports pair-level and rank-level judge agreement separately. Described from the project's own documentation; single author, no independent adoption yet.

**Tools** — [[llm-search-eval]] (LLM judge panels, paired statistics, CI gate)

## 2026-11-14 — The offline search evaluation tooling landscape (1 new, 3 updated)

Offline evaluation is a pipeline: sample queries, collect judgments, run systems, compute metrics, test the difference. No single tool covers it end to end. [[Offline Search Evaluation Tools]] maps six layers, from reference metric engines such as `trec_eval` and `ranx` and the PyTerrier experiment framework, through the [[Elasticsearch]] ranking evaluation API and workbenches like [[Quepid]] and [[Rated Ranking Evaluator]], to LLM judgment generators such as UMBRELA and RAG evaluators like [[RAGAS]]. The thin spots are clear: significance testing is found in research libraries but not in the workbenches, judge validation trails LLM judging, and stratified [[Query Sampling]] is barely tooled. Built from project repositories and documentation.

## 2026-11-14 — Alternative queries for zero results at Wallapop (3 new, 5 updated)

When a marketplace search returns nothing, you can drop one query term or swap a model number for its sibling. [[Rene Kriegler & Jean Silva - Alternative Queries for Zero Results]] follows up [[Rene Kriegler]]'s 2019 talk [[Rene Kriegler - Query Relaxation]] on Spanish queries. A fine-tuned RoBERTa classifier matches his old neural network at 0.98 accuracy once a token-order bug is fixed. A fill-mask model proposes replacement tokens, with mixed results. [[Wallapop]] shipped the simplest option instead: drop the rarest term. It scored about 80% offline against log-mined query pairs and was heading into an A/B test. [[Query Relaxation]] now covers token replacement and how to build that evaluation data. [[Haystack EU]] 2023 talk; speakers' own figures.

## 2026-11-13 — Query relaxation judged by conversion, not empty pages (3 new, 5 updated)

Dropping words until a search returns something can cut the [[Zero Results]] rate while still losing the sale. [[Aron Negyesi]]'s post, added as [[Query relaxation - the forgotten hero of eCommerce search]], recounts A/B tests at [[Rohlik]] where relaxed queries first converted at a fifth of the normal rate, then at over 60% after a better choice of which terms to drop. A spare-part search on a home improvement retailer's site shows the failure from outside: the rare brand and model code appear to have been discarded. [[Query Relaxation]] and [[Search UX]] now cover measuring relaxed-query conversion and showing shoppers which words were dropped. Medium member-only post; figures are the author's own.

## 2026-11-11 — Kendall, Spearman and Pearson side by side (0 new, 2 updated)

Judge papers quote several correlation coefficients for the same comparison, and the numbers are not interchangeable. [[Kendall Rank Correlation]] now sets Kendall's τ beside Spearman's ρ and Pearson's r: what each compares, what each is sensitive to, and why τ normally comes out lower than the other two on the same data. The PoLL jury reached Pearson 0.917 but τ 0.778 against the same Chatbot Arena leaderboard. Only τ should be held to the τ ≥ 0.9 equivalence bar. [[Replacing Judges with Juries]] now links its correlation figures to the comparison.

## 2026-11-11 — LLM juries, judge cascades and claim-level fact-checking (12 new, 7 updated)

Asking one large model for every verdict repeats its systematic errors and favours its own outputs. [[LLM Jury]] covers [[Cohere]]'s [[Replacing Judges with Juries|PoLL paper]], where three small judges from different model families agreed with human labels on KILT question answering better than GPT-4, at seven to eight times lower cost. [[Atomic Claim Verification]] checks long answers one fact at a time, following [[FActScore - Fine-grained Atomic Evaluation of Factual Precision|FActScore]] and [[Google]] DeepMind's search agent [[Long-form Factuality in Large Language Models|SAFE]]. [[Staged Judging]] now covers [[Trust or Escalate - LLM Judges with Provable Guarantees for Human Agreement|Trust or Escalate]], in which cheap judges pass unsure cases upward under a calibrated, provable human-agreement target. All figures are the papers' own.

**Corrections**
- [[LLM as Judge]] advised using a capable model (GPT-4, Claude) because cheaper ones calibrate worse → it now notes that GPT-4 underperformed small judges on reference-based QA, and that small judges can estimate their own reliability about as well as GPT-4.

## 2026-11-10 — LLM as Judges: A Vocabulary, published (1 new, 7 updated)

Agreement, self-consistency and calibration each make a separate, narrower claim about an AI relevance judge, and none of them means the judge is right. [[LLM as Judges - A Vocabulary]], [[Andrew Kornilov]]'s published glossary written from AI-as-judge work in [[Quepid]], defines nine such terms: the four [[Levels of Judge Agreement|levels of agreement]], inter-judge and ordinal-versus-binary agreement, self-consistency, qrels, adjudication, distilled students, two senses of [[Judge Calibration|calibration]], and juries. It opens with a short primer on the statistics it quotes, from Cohen's κ to macro F1. Much of its evidence comes from two 2026 arXiv papers, Better Retrievers, Worse Labels and CADRE.

## 2026-11-10 — Behavioural effects and biases in search (1 new, 5 updated)

Shoppers do not judge results one at a time. A pricey iPhone they click but never buy can make the cheaper phones beside it look better, [[Autocomplete]] suggestions anchor what people end up typing, and reviews pull clicks regardless of fit. [[Psychological and Behavioural Effects and Biases in Search]] gathers these choice effects with the biases in how results are examined ([[Position Bias]], trust bias, grid viewports) and in what gets logged ([[Presentation Bias]], [[Impression Bias]]). It also covers novelty effects in [[A-B Testing for Search|A/B tests]] and order and verbosity bias in [[LLM as Judge|LLM judges]]. It draws on [[Daniel Tunkelang]], on [[Roman Grebennikov]]'s curiosity clicks, and on [[Andreas Wagner]], who reads the same click-heavy, never-bought pattern as a possible decoy.

## 2026-11-10 — Products that sell their neighbours (0 new, 3 updated)

Some products draw many clicks and almost no sales, yet the products shown beside them seem to sell more. The latest iPhone gets the clicks, and the cheaper phones next to it start to look appealing. [[Andreas Wagner]]'s third post, now added to [[Why nDCG Misleads eCommerce Search]], links this to attention spillover, halo and decoy effects. It argues that [[Learning to Rank]] objectives which score each product on its own can pick a worse overall ranking. Two shoe grids with the same expert rating differ in one slot, and one is marked at 8% less GMV. Wagner notes the pattern is correlational and would need controlled experiments.

## 2026-11-09 — F1 in search, and macro vs micro averaging (2 new, 10 updated)

F1 scores a set, and search is judged by rankings, so most F1 figures in search measure a judge, classifier or router rather than the results page. [[Macro and Micro Averaging]] covers where that misleads. One overall F1 hides the hard boundaries: [[Allegro]]'s judge scored per-class F1 of 0.94 on exact matches but 0.33 on telling degrees of substitutability apart. On a literature-screening set, a [[GEPA]]-tuned prompt raised F1 ten points while deferring more true positives. Progressive scroll is the exception, where [[Doug Turnbull]] argues F1 over the whole result set fits better than [[NDCG]]. Macro, micro and weighted averaging follow the [[scikit-learn - Metrics and Scoring|scikit-learn user guide]]; micro-F1 equals accuracy in single-label tasks.

## 2026-11-09 — Why nDCG misleads eCommerce search (1 new, 9 updated)

Is nDCG the right number to optimise for product search? [[Andreas Wagner]] of [[searchHub]] argues not, in two LinkedIn posts now combined as [[Why nDCG Misleads eCommerce Search]]. Its log discount is assumed rather than measured, nDCG@5 or @10 ignores how far shoppers scroll, and product grids make attention drop in steps at row and viewport boundaries that differ between desktop and mobile, which a smooth curve cannot model. Its tie to revenue also weakens once images, prices, brands and promotions drive clicks. [[NDCG]] gains an eCommerce-limitations section and [[Position Bias]] now covers grid-shaped examination. [[Andrew Kornilov]]'s reply is recorded: teams use nDCG because nothing clearer is implementable.
