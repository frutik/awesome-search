# Dataset Types for Embedding Fine-Tuning

Embedding fine-tuning data comes in a small number of shapes, and the shape decides which loss can be used. [[Ritik Jain]] groups them into six. In practice the choice is rarely free: it is set by what labels already exist — query logs, human similarity scores, relevance judgments — and the loss follows from it (see [[Embedding Loss Functions]]).

---

## The six shapes

| Type | Record | Typical use | Loss | Suggested minimum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Positive pairs** | (anchor, positive) | semantic search, document retrieval, FAQ | MultipleNegativesRankingLoss | 2,000–5,000 pairs |
| **Triplets** | (anchor, positive, negative) | metric learning, ranking with explicit negatives | TripletLoss or MNRL | 1,000–5,000 triplets |
| **Similarity-score pairs (STS)** | (sentence 1, sentence 2, score 0–5) | sentence similarity, paraphrase | CoSENTLoss | 2,000–5,000 pairs |
| **Natural-language inference** | (premise, hypothesis, entailment / neutral / contradiction) | logical relationships | MNRL, after conversion to triplets | 10,000+ |
| **IR ranking** | (query, document, relevance grade), TREC qrels | document ranking, passage retrieval, QA | MNRL | 100+ queries over 10k–100k documents |
| **Hard negatives** | (query, positive, several hard negatives) | robustness, edge cases | MNRL | derived from the others |

The minimums are the author's guidance, not measured thresholds.

## What makes each one usable

- **Pairs** — the positive must actually be relevant; no exact duplicates; anchor and positive lengths kept in a sensible range; one domain throughout.
- **Triplets** — the negative should be clearly wrong yet ideally hard; no overlap between positive and negative; two to five negatives per anchor.
- **STS pairs** — scores from human annotators rather than generated, with inter-annotator agreement of at least 0.8 and a spread of scores across the scale rather than clusters at the ends.
- **NLI** — the three labels in roughly equal proportion, agreement above 0.7, no ambiguous examples.
- **IR ranking** — queries that reflect real user intent, judgments with agreement above 0.7, a balance of relevant and non-relevant documents, and **no query shared between train and test**. That last rule is the one that most often inflates results — see [[Model Selection and Fine-Tuning Evaluation]].
- **Hard negatives** — genuinely difficult but never secretly correct, kept separate between train and test, and cheap enough to generate at scale.

## Where hard negatives come from

The author lists five strategies: top-k results from [[BM25]] or the current model, statements that contradict the answer, partially correct but incomplete answers, same-topic text from the wrong domain, and a curriculum that starts easy and gets harder. The false-negative risk — a "negative" that is actually relevant — is covered in [[Hard Negative Mining]].

## How these relate to other sources of labels

IR ranking data is the same material as [[Judgment Lists]]. Positive pairs are often mined from behaviour — clicks or purchases treated as positives, see [[Implicit Judgments]] — or generated by an LLM to bootstrap ([[Embedding Fine-tuning]] compares the construction methods). What is chosen as a positive defines what "relevant" means to the resulting model ([[Contrastive Learning]]).
